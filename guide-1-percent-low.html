1% low and 0.1% low FPS: what they mean and why average FPS misleads

A plain explanation of 1% low and 0.1% low, how different tools compute them and how to compare two setups fairly.

Average FPS tells how many frames come out per second on average. It does not tell how evenly they come. You can have 700 FPS on average and visible stutters.

1% low shows the slowest frames: the frame rate you would get if every frame were as slow as the worst 1%. 0.1% low is the same for the worst thousandth of frames, the rarest and most visible stutters. The closer the 1% and 0.1% low are to the average, the smoother the game.

Tools compute it differently

  • Percentile: take the frame time that only 1% of frames exceed and turn it into FPS (1000 / time in ms). Toolbox and DomainSchedTest do this.
  • Mean of the worst: take every frame in the worst 1% and average them. CapFrameX does this in its "1% Low".

Never compare numbers from different tools: the same recording gives different values. Compare runs of one tool, computed one way.

How to compare two setups fairly

  • Change one thing: the one you compare. Same PC, drivers and game settings.
  • The same number of runs for each setup, for example 3 and 3. One number proves nothing: live play is noisy.
  • Look at the ranges. If the ranges of two setups overlap, the difference is noise.

An example from the developer's PC (Dust2 benchmark, CapFrameX, 2 runs each): without Toolbox the 1% low was 271 D1, with a Toolbox 1.16.0 test build 286 D3. The average FPS barely moved: 700 and 715. This is why we look at the 1% and 0.1% low, not at the average.

What to measure with

  • Toolbox, the Check page: one button, 30 seconds, a report on the desktop: FPS, 1% and 0.1% low, the longest frame, stutters, the game threads' waits and a hint about what limits the frame rate. Toolbox is free and runs on any Windows 10/11.
  • DomainSchedTest: several runs in a row and one table with means and ranges. The guide is on the Measure page.
